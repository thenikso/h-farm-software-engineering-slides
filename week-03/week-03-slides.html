Explanation5 minWeek 03

Stakeholders, elicitation, and scope

If the client asks for a feature, is that the requirement?

Trace an answer to a project decision.

Textbook study

§4.3, pp. 112-116. Printed pages in Sommerville (2016), 10th edition, Global Edition. See the session 3 reading guide for the study question and optional selections.

Session question

A feature request is evidence of a need, not automatically a settled requirement. We will trace one question through to a project decision.

Say · rehearsal script

Last week we made a recommendation about how to organise the work. That recommendation depended on things we still needed to find out. Today we will follow one of those questions far enough to see what its answer actually lets us decide. A sponsor asks for reminders. We can understand the request and still need to ask what problem the sponsor expects reminders to solve.

By the end, you should be able to show how a stakeholder answer supports a project decision. Keep your original wording beside the later version. That will help us see whether we learned something useful, and where we are still making assumptions. This is practice for the contextual part of Software Project Framing.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Transition: start with the students’ Week 2 recommendations. Do not require everyone to choose reminders.

Your turn8 min · write, pair, report

A confident request

“We need a reminder feature.” What is still unknown?

Write two interpretations. Compare with a partner, then share one difference.

Purpose

The same feature label can hide different outcomes, users, timing needs, access needs, and operational constraints.

Say · rehearsal script

Imagine a client says, “We need a reminder feature.” It sounds quite clear. But how much do we really know about what they want to improve?

First write two possible meanings on your own, then compare them with your partner. Choose one difference between your interpretations that would matter to what we build. I will ask a few pairs to share that difference, so keep a sentence ready. We are collecting possibilities here; we have not heard the client’s explanation yet.

Ask after pair comparison

Which difference between your interpretations would change what we build?

Response guidance · not spoken

Hear two pairs from different parts of the room. Keep their interpretations provisional; do not choose a model answer.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Within the eight minutes, allow two minutes to write, three to compare, and three for sampled reporting. Invite pairs from different parts of the room; allow a student to read their sentence or have their partner report. Do not volunteer interpretations before the attempt.

Explanation8 min

Two overlapping groups

User

Interacts directly with the system.

Stakeholder

Is affected by it or can shape its constraints and success.

A person can be both. Some important stakeholders are not users.

Useful distinction

The labels help us notice missing perspectives. They are not a hierarchy of whose needs automatically win.

Say · rehearsal script

A student who reserves a place is using the service directly. The organiser may also use it to check attendance. Both have a stake in whether it works. But someone can be affected without opening the booking screen: think of the person responsible for planning the room from the attendance information.

A stakeholder is someone affected by the system or able to shape its constraints and success. Users are part of that group. The distinction helps us notice people we might otherwise leave out of the conversation. It does not tell us whose view should automatically win.

Ask the room

Whose concern might we miss if we spoke only to students making reservations?

Response guidance · not spoken

Accept a named role plus a consequence. Probe a role label without a reason.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Use the room-planning example as an illustration, not an additional fact in the supplied evidence. Check for the misconception that only managers are stakeholders.

Explanation8 min

Do not collapse the evidence

NeedOutcome or problem
FeatureProposed response
ConstraintLimit on the response
AssumptionUnconfirmed belief

Why separate them

A feature may address a real need, but it can also embed an unsupported solution or ignore a constraint.

Say · rehearsal script

Let’s separate four things that can easily end up in the same sentence. Someone needs to know whether they have a place. Someone proposes an email reminder. The event may have a fixed date. And we might be assuming that everyone reads the email in time.

The need is the outcome someone needs; the feature is one proposed way of helping them reach it. The fixed date limits our options. The claim about reading email still needs evidence. These are illustrative examples; we will now check what the actual statements support.

We can take a feature request seriously while asking why it would help. Otherwise we risk building exactly what was requested and leaving the original problem unresolved.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Do not present the illustrative email assumption as an established Campus Event fact. Transition to page 1 and its actual wording.

Explanation10 min

A consequential question

Ask for evidence that could change a decision

Target

One known uncertainty.

Answerable

A stakeholder can respond from their knowledge.

Consequence

The answer could alter scope, priority, or wording.

Question test

A broad invitation can start a conversation, but today’s question must support an inspectable reasoning chain.

Say · rehearsal script

Before the break, let’s look at what makes a question worth asking. It needs a particular uncertainty, someone with relevant knowledge, and a decision that the answer could affect. “Would you like reminders?” may get agreement without telling us much about the problem.

For example, we could ask a student what they need to know immediately after pressing Reserve. Their answer might help us decide what information to show at that point. It would not establish whether the reservation was actually saved; that needs a technical check.

Imagine two plausible answers and explain what you would do differently in each case. This is a way to test the question before spending someone’s time on it. Keep your predictions separate from what the stakeholder later says.

Ask the room

What could a student’s account tell us about the reservation journey, and what would still need a technical check?

Response guidance · not spoken

After an attempt: the account can describe the response they perceived; it cannot by itself establish whether a reservation was stored.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Use one sound move and one gap from circulation. Keep this worked question distinct from the question students will choose. Shorten further examples before cutting their writing time.

Your turn15 min · pairs

Choose one voice

Draft one question worth answering

Write the stakeholder, the question, the decision it could change, and what two different answers might imply.

Output

Say · rehearsal script

With your partner, choose the stakeholder whose answer could help with your decision. On page 2, write one question, the decision it might change, and two possible answers that would lead you in different directions.

Give the stakeholder room to say something that challenges your plan. If your question already contains the answer you want, try asking about their experience or the outcome they need. Check that this person could know the answer. A student can describe what they saw; they may not know what the database recorded.

Ask if needed

What would you decide differently after each of your two possible answers?

Response guidance · not spoken

If both answers leave every decision unchanged, help the pair identify a consequential uncertainty without supplying a question.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Circulate to three pairs, including a pair that has not reported aloud. Inspect the consequence beside the question. At seven minutes, ask unfinished pairs to name the decision; at twelve, ask them to check their two answer directions.

Your turn10 min · neighbouring pairs

Before you ask

Would the answer actually change anything?

Challenge the target, wording, or consequence. Revise one part.

Feedback focus

Check whether the stakeholder can answer, whether the question exposes uncertainty, and whether the answer could change a bounded decision.

Say · rehearsal script

Pass your question to the nearest pair. Read theirs before they explain it, and say what decision you think the answer would help them make.

Choose one useful challenge: who could answer it, how it is worded, or what the answer could change. Then return the note. Authors, use that feedback to improve one part. You can keep your wording if you can explain why it already does the job.

Ask if needed

What decision do you think this question is intended to help them make?

Response guidance · not spoken

Let the reader answer before the authors explain. A mismatch may reveal wording or an unstated assumption.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Keep neighbouring pairs together. Visit one exchange and listen for a question about the decision, rather than a preference about phrasing. Shorten this exchange if needed to protect the preserved question and answer-led revision.

Pause5 min

Preserve the question

Commit before the answer arrives

Write your final question and predicted decision consequence.

Why preserve it

The question and prediction make the effect of the later answer visible. Do not rewrite the question after seeing the prepared response.

Say · rehearsal script

Finish your pre-answer version on page 2. Keep the question and the decision you expected it to affect visible.

Do not replace this question after seeing the answer. You may later discover a better question. We will label that as a follow-up, so another reader can still see what you asked and what you learned from it.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Check the commitment is visible before supplying a response. If a pair is stuck, preserve their best current question and its uncertainty; do not write the question for them.

Explanation5 min

The second half

What does the answer let us decide?

Record what was said before deciding what it means.

Reasoning boundary

Separate the stakeholder answer from the engineering interpretation. The answer may support more than one defensible revision.

Say · rehearsal script

We are about to move from asking to interpreting. First record what the stakeholder actually says. Then explain what you think follows for the project.

The stakeholder’s answer and our engineering decision are two different parts of the note. An answer may justify changing a decision, adding a condition, or retaining it with a clearer reason. We need to show that connection. We also need to say what this person’s answer cannot settle.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Accept a retained decision only when the learner explains how the answer supports it. Do not force an arbitrary change to satisfy the worksheet wording.

Pause5 min

Prepared stakeholder response

Receive the answer mapped to your question

Copy it accurately. Do not improve it.

Answer route

The facilitator provides a prepared answer matched to the selected concern. Live role-play is optional, not required.

Say · rehearsal script

Show me the question you preserved. I will give you the prepared response that matches the concern you are asking about. On page 3, record its identifier, the stakeholder, and the exact wording, or attach the extract.

If the answer leaves part of your question open, mark that gap. Please do not fill it with something the person might have meant. If the available record does not answer your question, we can identify the next enquiry instead of inventing a response.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Release pages 3–4 and only the matched answer from instructor-guide.md. Match by decision, not keywords. For a large class, group matching questions and read the relevant response verbatim so the five-minute gate is feasible. Supply a second response only after a consequential follow-up. Never distribute the whole guide.

Your turn10 min · on your own

One evidence chain

QuestionWhat you asked
AnswerWhat you learned
RevisionWhat changes
UnknownWhat remains open

Complete all four in your project-frame note.

Essential Week 3 evidence

The note must show the question, stakeholder answer, revised or explicitly supported objective, boundary, requirement direction, or scope decision, and one remaining conflict or unknown.

Say · rehearsal script

Work individually on page 3. Keep your question beside the response and write the project decision it supports. What would you now do, exclude, clarify, or investigate? Then begin the remaining-unknown field on page 4.

Point to the words in the answer that support your decision. Repeating the answer tells us what the stakeholder said. We also need your reasoning about what to do with it. If you retain your earlier decision, explain what the answer now gives you grounds to say.

Ask while circulating

Which sentence about the project can you now justify, and which words in the answer support it?

Response guidance · not spoken

A copied answer needs an interpretation. A retained decision needs an explicit reason; change for its own sake is unnecessary.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Circulate to three individual notes. Inspect the answer-to-decision link before helping with wording. Keep one grounded revision and one unsupported leap for the comparison on the next slide; anonymise both.

Explanation10 min

The answer can move different parts

Change

Objective, scope boundary, requirement direction, priority, or assumption.

Preserve

A conflict, dependency, ambiguity, or unanswered question.

No forced certainty

A strong frame uses what is known and makes consequential uncertainty visible. It does not pretend one interview completes elicitation.

Say · rehearsal script

Let’s compare what happened in your notes. An answer can lead to a smaller scope, a clearer condition, or a question we need to settle before promising a feature. It can also give us a reason to keep the decision we already had.

A useful answer changes what we can justify, even when we keep the same decision. Listen to the two examples we have just seen: which words support the decision, and where does the author go further than the answer allows?

One person’s answer also has limits. An organiser can agree an event rule, but that does not establish how the software currently behaves. We carry the unresolved part forward so the next person knows what still needs checking.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Use anonymised work sampled on slide 15, with permission before reading a student’s wording. If no usable sample is available, ask for one pair to read their answer and decision. Keep private worked alternatives in the instructor guide; do not project the response map.

Make change visible

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Circulate to three notes and inspect an actual before-and-after sentence. At ten minutes, ask anyone still planning to write the sentence. Your turn10 min · pairs

Do not close the case

What consequential conflict or unknown survives this answer?

Name it and state the next evidence or decision needed.

Useful uncertainty

The remaining issue should affect scope, priority, quality, delivery, or stakeholder alignment. “More research is needed” is not enough.

Say · rehearsal script

Compare your remaining unknown with your partner. What decision could it still affect? Then name the next evidence or agreement you would seek, and the person who could help.

Make the next step specific enough that someone could act on it. “More research” does not say where to begin. We need to know what we are trying to establish. That might call for another stakeholder conversation, a technical check, or an agreement between people with conflicting expectations.

Ask if needed

What would the next conversation or check establish that this answer did not?

Response guidance · not spoken

Keep the next step tied to a specific decision. Another stakeholder cannot supply evidence about an unobserved implementation.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Visit pairs with different response categories. Look for a feasible next step that addresses the named unknown. Do not demand a numerical target or technical diagnosis absent from the evidence.

Feedback protocol

Partner points to the evidence link, identifies any unsupported leap, and asks one question about the remaining unknown.

Say · rehearsal script

Exchange your complete note. Read your partner’s answer and decision before asking them to explain. Point to the evidence link, identify any unsupported leap you find, and ask one question about the remaining uncertainty.

Give feedback about what the evidence supports. If the reasoning is sound, say which link convinced you; there is no need to invent a flaw. Authors, listen first and record the feedback on page 4. You will have time to decide what to use.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Use four minutes for each reader, then four minutes to record feedback and a possible revision. Circulate and check that comments concern the reasoning. Do not let the most confident student rewrite their partner’s work.

Your turn13 min · on your own

Use the feedback

Strengthen one weak link

Strengthen the evidence link, decision, or remaining unknown. Preserve your original question.

One revision is enough

The aim is to use feedback before Week 4, not to perfect an entire project frame.

Say · rehearsal script

Use the feedback to strengthen one part of your own note. You might make the scope clearer, explain the evidence link, or identify a more useful next enquiry. Mark the change on page 4 and explain why you made it.

Keep the original question and stakeholder answer intact. If you have thought of a better question, label it as a follow-up. If you reject a suggestion, explain why your decision is still supported. The reader should be able to follow that reasoning too.

Ask if needed

Which connection in your note would be hardest for another reader to follow?

Response guidance · not spoken

Let the learner choose the weak link, then ask for the smallest useful revision.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

Protect this individual revision time. Make a short circuit to check that students are revising their own work and recording why. If someone is stuck, ask them to choose the least convincing connection in the note.
