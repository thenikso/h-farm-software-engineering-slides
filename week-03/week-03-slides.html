Explanation4 min

Two overlapping groups

User

Interacts directly with the system.

Stakeholder

Is affected by it or can shape its constraints and success.

A person can be both. Some important stakeholders are not users.

Useful distinction

The labels help us notice missing perspectives. They are not a hierarchy of whose needs automatically win.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

A stakeholder is someone affected by the system or able to shape its constraints and success.

Say · rehearsal script

A student who reserves a place is using the service directly. The organiser may also use it to check attendance. Both have a stake in whether it works. But someone can be affected without opening the booking screen: think of the person responsible for planning the room from the attendance information.

A stakeholder is someone affected by the system or able to shape its constraints and success. Users are part of that group. The distinction helps us notice people we might otherwise leave out of the conversation. It does not tell us whose view should automatically win.

Ask the room

Whose concern might we miss if we spoke only to students making reservations?

Tutor reference · not spoken, not for students

e.g. Support, who are asked “am I booked?” and should not have to guess from an email screenshot; or the person planning the room from attendance figures that may be wrong. Each is a role who need not open the booking screen, plus a consequence; the organiser is also sound, though also a user.

Response guidance · not spoken

Target: someone who may never open the booking screen, plus a consequence, rather than the sponsor. Accept a named role plus a consequence. Probe a role label without a reason.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

  • About a minute to say this, then up to three for the question: one or two sampled answers, probing a bare role for its consequence.
  • The room-planning example is illustrative; it is not in today’s statements.
  • Listen for two misconceptions: only managers count as stakeholders, and “the sponsor pays, so their request has to go in”.

Hint or correction: If the answer only names a role, ask what consequence or decision makes that role relevant. If a student says “the sponsor pays, so their request has to go in”: the sponsor shapes success and constraints; that makes their need important, not their proposed feature automatically correct. Ask: what else could they need that reminders might not deliver?

Explanation5 min

Do not collapse the evidence

NeedOutcome or problem
FeatureProposed response
ConstraintLimit on the response
AssumptionUnconfirmed belief

Why separate them

A feature may address a real need, but it can also embed an unsupported solution or ignore a constraint.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

The need is the outcome someone needs; the feature is one proposed way of helping them reach it.

Say · rehearsal script

Let’s separate four things that can easily end up in the same sentence.

Someone needs to know whether they have a place. Someone proposes an email reminder. The event may have a fixed date. And we might be assuming that everyone reads the email in time.

The need is the outcome someone needs; the feature is one proposed way of helping them reach it. The fixed date limits our options. The claim about reading email still needs evidence. These are illustrative examples; we will now check what the actual statements support.

We can take a feature request seriously while asking why it would help. Otherwise we risk building exactly what was requested and leaving the original problem unresolved.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

  • About a minute to say this, then up to three for the question and one probe; after an attempt, share the gist of the tutor reference.
  • Leave the last minute for the Part A handout.
  • These four examples are made up to illustrate the terms; don’t present them as Campus Event facts.
  • Then hand out Part A pages 1–2 as you move to slide 6 and the real statements.

Explanation10 min

A consequential question

Ask for evidence that could change a decision

Target

One known uncertainty.

Answerable

A stakeholder can respond from their knowledge.

Consequence

The answer could alter scope, priority, or wording.

Question test

A broad invitation can start a conversation, but today’s question must support an inspectable reasoning chain.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

Imagine two plausible answers and explain what you would do differently in each case.

Say · rehearsal script

Before the break, let’s look at what makes a question worth asking. It needs a particular uncertainty, someone with relevant knowledge, and a decision that the answer could affect. Take an example from outside our case: a bus stop with an arrival display. Asking a passenger “Would you like a bigger display?” may get agreement without telling us much about the problem.

Instead, we could ask a regular passenger what they decide when they look at the display. Their answer might help us decide what the display should show first. It would not tell us everything about how the display works.

Imagine two plausible answers and explain what you would do differently in each case. This is a way to test the question before spending someone’s time on it. Keep your predictions separate from what the stakeholder later says.

Ask the room

What could a passenger’s account tell us about the display, and what would still need a technical check?

Tutor reference · not spoken, not for students

A passenger can describe what they saw and did: a bus shown as “2 min” that never came, giving up and walking, no longer trusting the display. Whether the times come from live tracking or the printed timetable, and why one was wrong, needs a technical check.

Response guidance · not spoken

Their report supports investigating the experience, not diagnosing the implementation.

Then ask · same worked question

Imagine two plausible answers from that passenger. What would we do differently in each case?

Tutor reference · not spoken, not for students

e.g. “Whether to wait or walk to another stop” makes an accurate next-arrival time the priority, so investigate where the times come from; “which bus goes to my destination” points to route and destination information instead, and a bigger screen may not matter. Both are predictions, not what a passenger said; neither shows why a time was wrong.

Response guidance · not spoken

Take one pair of answers. If both answers lead to the same action, ask whether the question can change anything.

Delivery guidance · not spoken

  • About a minute to share what you saw on slide 7, under two for the script, up to five for the first question and about two for the two-answers follow-up.
  • If the first answer blurs what a passenger can observe with what a technical check establishes, take a second before sharing the gist of the tutor reference.
  • If time is short, share the follow-up reference yourself rather than running over 1:00.
  • Open by sharing one good move and one common gap you saw on slide 7, without names.
  • Keep this worked question separate from the questions students will write; it is deliberately outside Campus Event.
  • Shorten further examples before cutting their writing time.

Hint or correction: If someone suggests asking the passenger whether the tracking system failed, ask what a passenger can actually observe.
