03

Repair Desk

A fictional practice case. The first midterm uses a different case with the same six boxes.

A community repair workshop fixes small household items. Repair requests arrive on paper and by email. Staff sometimes copy the same request twice or lose the note that says an item is ready, so residents travel to the workshop without knowing whether they can collect it.

The coordinator wants a browser-based repair register for the two shared desk computers at the workshop’s one site. Desk volunteers create a record with a unique reference, the resident’s name and contact detail, an item description and the date received. Technicians add a short update and set the status: received, being repaired, ready for collection, or collected. Volunteers use the record to answer residents’ enquiries. Only authorised volunteers and technicians may see records; residents do not log in.

Public status lookup, payments, parts ordering and technician scheduling are not part of the first release.

For the pilot, the coordinator proposes that at least 9 of 10 reference lookups show the matching record within 3 seconds, with 500 records stored and both desk computers in use. Creating and looking up a record must also work by keyboard alone, with labelled fields. Four volunteers will be trained before the pilot.

The coordinator wants residents told when an item is ready. Volunteers disagree about who should contact residents and whether the register should send messages automatically. Nobody has decided how long contact details are kept.

Practice conditions · 60 minutes · on your own

  • Suggested: 5 minutes to read, 10 to plan, 35 to write, 10 to check.
  • Aim for about 300 words in total. Bullets are fine. The printed starters don’t count.
  • Three functional and two non-functional requirements are enough.
  • Use only this case. Not graded: it is practice for the first midterm.

08

One good Repair Desk memo

One way to answer, about 300 words. Other answers can be just as good if they link the boxes the same way. Use it to study, not to copy.

1The problem is that repair requests are copied twice or lost between paper and email, so residents travel without knowing whether their item is ready. The main users are desk volunteers, who record requests and answer enquiries, and technicians, who update progress. Residents are stakeholders who need accurate collection information; the coordinator sets the rules.

2The first release aims to give authorised staff one shared, current record of each repair. It includes creating, updating and looking up records on the two desk computers. It does not include public lookup, payments, parts ordering, scheduling or automatic messages to residents, because the contact decision is still open.

3FR1: A desk volunteer can create a repair record with the supplied details and a unique reference, so that each request is recorded once.
FR2: A technician can set a repair’s status and add a short update, so that the desk sees current progress.
FR3: A volunteer can look up a record by its reference and see its status, so that they can tell a resident whether the item is ready.

4NFR1 (speed): at least 9 of 10 reference lookups show the matching record within 3 seconds, with 500 records stored and both desk computers in use. Proposed by the coordinator; to confirm in the pilot.
NFR2 (accessibility): creating and looking up a record can be done by keyboard alone, with labelled fields. Supplied.

5Tests FR3: Given a record RD-104 with status “ready for collection”, when a volunteer looks up RD-104, then the register shows that record with status “ready for collection”.

6Not yet decided: who tells residents that an item is ready, and whether the register sends messages. It matters because automatic messages would settle a disagreement between volunteers and need rules for contact details. Before development, the coordinator must decide who contacts residents and how long contact details are kept.

Why it works: each FR says “so that” and points back to the problem in box 1; the speed target keeps its conditions and says who proposed it; box 5 tests FR3, which is written above; box 6 keeps the contact question open instead of quietly building messaging.
