Explanation

A working distinction

Uncertainty

What the team does not yet know.

Risk

Something that could happen and the consequence if it does.

Planning can expose uncertainty. Evidence is often needed to reduce it.

Use these as working terms

The distinction helps us ask whether a plan settles an issue, merely records an assumption, or creates a route to obtain evidence.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

A plan makes an assumption visible; evidence helps test it.

Say · rehearsal script

Uncertainty is something relevant that we do not yet know; risk concerns what could happen and why it would matter. Think back to Campus Event before launch. An uncertainty was whether pressing Reserve again after no confirmation would create another reservation. A related risk was that a retry could create duplicate bookings, leaving the organiser with an unreliable reservation list. The uncertainty is the unanswered question; the risk is the possible outcome and its consequence.

Planning can identify the assumption, make someone responsible for investigating it, and reserve time to respond. But the plan cannot make the assumption true. Think of your Week 1 causal strip: separating evidence from assumptions showed which claims still needed checking. From an assumption, we can plan a useful check, decide what its result would let us conclude, and leave time to respond. We may need to inspect, ask, try something, or run a check. That is how planning and evidence work together.

Explanation

Ways to organise the work

Plan-driven

Agree substantial scope and sequence before implementation.

Iterative

Revisit and improve a solution using what each cycle reveals.

Incremental

Deliver useful parts in stages and extend what the system can do.

A real project may combine them.

These are choices, not team identities

The useful question is which approach fits a part of the work under its current conditions. Teams often plan some dependencies, iterate on uncertain behaviour, and deliver in increments.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

Plan commitments, improve versions, and deliver useful capability.

Say · rehearsal script

Plan-driven work establishes substantial scope and sequence early. It is useful when people need to coordinate dependencies and commitments. The plan may still include reviews and changes; planning does not mean refusing feedback.

Iteration means revisiting a solution using evidence. We try a journey, learn where it is unclear, revise it, and examine it again. We are improving the same solution.

An increment adds useful capability. A connected booking service could be one useful part, with automated reminders added later. A project can plan the commitments, iterate on uncertain behaviour, and deliver useful parts separately. We will look at what makes each choice worthwhile.

These choices answer different questions and can coexist. Planning asks which commitments and dependencies to agree early. Iteration asks what to revise after learning. Increments ask which useful capability can become available separately.

Explanation

Plan-driven work

Useful when late change is costly and dependencies are knowable

The plan coordinates commitments. It also depends on the quality of early information.

Conditions that strengthen the case

Stable constraints, expensive rework, external approvals, fixed interfaces, or a dependency order that the team can establish early.

What it may leave exposed

If user behaviour or technical feasibility remains uncertain, a detailed plan can preserve a wrong assumption for longer.

Anchor · if you lose the thread

Say · rehearsal script

The limitation is the information behind the plan. A detailed plan can preserve a wrong assumption if nobody checks it until late. So a plan-driven approach still needs evidence at points where important decisions can be reconsidered.

Explanation

Iterative work

Useful when feedback can improve the same solution

Each cycle should answer a useful question, not merely repeat activity.

Conditions that strengthen the case

What it may leave exposed

We can keep polishing the same journey and still have nothing usable to release. Before another round, decide what we need to learn and when we have enough evidence to move on.

Explanation

Incremental work

Useful when a smaller part can provide value and evidence

An increment changes what the system can usefully do.

Conditions that strengthen the case

The first part lets someone complete a useful task safely, while another capability can wait. Any work left for later needs a workable arrangement in the meantime.

What it may leave exposed

A small release can still leave the difficult connections undone. Check whether the first part actually works for the people meant to use it.

Explanation

1970 · large software systems

Late feedback was already recognised as a serious risk

Winston Royce described a simple sequential process as risky, then added feedback, early modelling, documentation, testing, and customer involvement.

The historical connection

Royce’s paper responded to the difficulty of managing large software systems when problems discovered late forced expensive returns to earlier decisions.

Why it matters today

The paper does not give us one timeless method. It shows that engineering practices respond to concrete coordination and quality problems.

Sources

Winston W. Royce, “Managing the Development of Large Software Systems”, 1970 (open reprint); Royce portrait, 1990 (public domain).

Say · rehearsal script

In 1970, Winston Royce discussed the development of large software systems. His concern included discovering practical limitations late, when testing could send a team back to change earlier design decisions.

His response included early modelling and customer involvement alongside planned stages, documentation, and testing. Feedback and planning were both part of the response. This gives us a historical example of practices addressing a concrete engineering problem.

AI might help us write the booking flow quickly. But if we wait until after launch to learn that students misunderstand the confirmation, we still have to check their bookings, change the message, and explain what happened. Writing the code faster did not bring that feedback forward.

For today, remember the connection: seek evidence before a commitment becomes expensive to change. You do not need to memorise the date or treat the paper as a universal method. We will apply that connection to our own recommendation.

Quick vocabulary · if asked

An uncertainty is something that matters to the plan but that we do not yet know. For example, the team does not yet know how much work the booking repair needs.

A constraint is a limit the team has to work within. The event date is fixed, even though the sponsor could reduce scope or postpone online booking.

A dependency is when one activity or decision needs something from another first. Before real students rely on a booking outcome, the team needs a connected check that booking, notifications, and organiser information agree.

A question is what you would ask or check to reduce a particular uncertainty. For example: “What work is needed to correct the booking behaviour?” The answer could affect what the team promises for this event. Students can write the question without inventing its answer.
